*Originally published on my defunct substack on August 30, 2025.*

A month ago, the whole AI world was discussing International Mathematical Olympiad. While it is interesting and important, there is another olympiad, more suited for language models: the [International Linguistics Olympiad](https://ioling.org/) or IOL. Linguistic problems that don't require language knowledge, but just a sense of what languages are and how they work, were invented by the Soviet linguist [Andrey Zaliznyak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Zaliznyak) in 1962. The first problems were much easier than modern. The first problem in the book is this one:

## A problem from 1962

A text of 12 phrases in an unknown language (Basque) is given. It is known that one of the phrases is grammatically incorrect due to an error in one word (in a more strict form: due to the fact that in one case one sequence of letters between spaces is replaced by some other sequence of letters).

1. Gizona joaten da.
2. Gizonak zaldia ikusten du.
3. Astoa atzo joaten zan.
4. Gizonak atzo joaten ziran.
5. Astoak zaldiak atzo ikusten zuen.
6. Zaldiak gizona ikusten du.
7. Zakurrak joaten dira.
8. Gizonak zakurra atzo ikusten zuen.
9. Zakurrak astoak ikusten ditu.
10. Zaldiak gizonak atzo ikusten zituen.
11. Zakurra atzo joaten zan.
12. Gizonak astoak atzo ikusten zituen.

**Task:** Find a grammatically incorrect phrase and make it grammatically correct by changing (or replacing) only one word in it.

[The book with these problems](https://old.mccme.ru/free-books/aaz/aaz-2013-3.pdf) is in Russian and freely available on the web. Though I’m not a linguist and never studied linguistics, I was able to solve 4 problems, each under an hour. Primarily because of that, I thought that LLMs would easily solve such problems.

LLMs have many advantages over humans: they amassed a lot of language knowledge and saw more text than anyone can in their whole life. While a human can use logic to determine the error in Basque sentences, an LLM can just spot the error as it can write grammatically correct Basque. That’s in theory. In practice, even the Zaliznyak's problems from 1962, freely available on the web, and surely in the training data, present significant challenges. The Basque problem, number 1 in Zaliznyak's book, was easily solved by Claude 4 Sonnet and Opus in the extended thinking mode, ChatGPT o3 and 5 (in any mode), while Claude Opus 4.1, Sonnet 4 and ChatGPT 4o failed to spot an error, or “spotted” multiple errors. (The correct answer is: sentence 5.)

## The 2025 Olympiad

Modern IOL has much, much more difficult problems. In each of 5 problems, you should parse some text in a low-resource language that you don’t know, and then answer multiple questions, translate multiple phrases, etc, so the chance of random guessing is almost zero. This year, problem 5 involved images, and so for LLMs the problem is multimodal - it’s not enough to process text.

Part of problem 5:

![Four illustrations of people acting out Kaqchikel sentences in IOL 2025 problem 5.](/assets/img/iol-2025-problem-5.webp)

I failed to solve these problems, but I must admit I spent only few hours on the first two. What about LLMs? Because non-reasoning models failed on several old problems, I tested only largest available reasoners and ChatGPT 4.5 (I run my tests before it was removed from Plus subscription). I didn’t cherry-pick answers, and I asked each question in a new chat with a simple prompt:

```text
You are a participant of the International Linguistics Olympiad 2025.

Unless stated differently, you should describe any patterns or rules that you identified in the data. Otherwise your solution will not be awarded full marks.

You can't use web search.

That's your first problem, solve it.
```

I re-run several questions multiple times when I got an error or hit usage limits (hey, Anthropic, two problems for Opus 4.1 are very low limits!) Only once GPT-5 refused to answer, stating that there are not enough data. Otherwise, I recorded the first full answer by a model.

I’m not a judge of the IOL and I don’t know how exactly the results are counted, so I just counted the number of questions per problem, and then the number of correct answers for each LLM.

## Results

First, the results, then some thoughts:

| Model | Problem 1 | Problem 2 | Problem 3 | Problem 4 | Problem 5 | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Claude Opus 4.1, extended thinking | 2 | 20 | 6 | 23 | 2 | 53 |
| GPT-5 Thinking | 0 | 6 | 8 | 23 | 10 | 47 |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro | 0 | 1 | 9 | 26 | 7 | 43 |
| GPT-4.5 | 0 | 1 | 3 | 17 | 1 | 22 |
| Questions per problem | 16 | 32 | 10 | 39 | 13 | 110 |

All models failed to solve at least one problem fully. Problem 1 is the hardest, and involves work with numbers in two systems in Dzongkha language.

Problem 2 was very hard for both GPT and Gemini, but half-solved by Claude. It’s about the Gaahmg language from Sudan.

Problems 3 and 4 were solved for ~50%; problem 3 is about the Kuria language from Kenya and Tanzania, and problem 4 is about the Kewa language from Papua New Guinea.

And finally, multimodal problem 5. While GPT-5 was the best here, it failed on one important question, which asked “Draw all the possible pictures that the following sentences could describe, or write equivalent verbal descriptions” (emphasis mine). All models except Gemini proposed one solution, while Gemini correctly described two. Claude, surprisingly, showed the worst results here. The problem is about the Kaqchikel language from Guatemala.

GPT-4.5 is the largest non-reasoning model, and its results show that reasoning works pretty well for all other models.

It’s very interesting why some problems were so much harder to solve than the others. I doubt that Claude has a lot of Gaahmg texts in its training data, or that GPT-5 saw more Kaqchikel than other models. Can it be generalization on similar languages? I doubt it. The questions are very specific, and even if a model was generalizing, it’s not enough data to know tones and accents in a low-resource language.

| Language | Speakers | Available texts |
|---|---|---|
| Dzongkha | 170k–600k | 22 entries at Glottolog, 119 entries at OLAC, Wikipedia |
| Gaahmg | ~100k | 46 entries at Glottolog, 18 entries at OLAC |
| Kuria | 500k–700k | 54 entries at Glottolog, 7 entries at OLAC |
| Kewa | 100k | 56 entries at Glottolog, 104 entries at OLAC, SIL dictionary |
| Kaqchikel | 400k–500k | 127 entries at Glottolog, 117 entries at OLAC, Test Wikipedia |

Even though grammars, dictionaries, and other publications exist for all these languages, these lists of publications are of course not the lists of materials in LLM training data, and it doesn’t reflect language’s usage on the Internet. It also doesn’t correlate with models’ performance on the problems.

## Conclusion

All models are way too far from gold. I don’t think any can even win bronze. While their reasoning helps a lot on some problems, it’s surprisingly uneven. The questions are specifically designed around low-resource languages, and participants are expected not to know the languages. LLMs are in the same situation, but they mostly can’t reason with the very limited data given in the problems. The IMO problems were solved by specially trained models, which tried to solve each problem multiple times, not the commercially available ones, so it’s not a real comparison.
